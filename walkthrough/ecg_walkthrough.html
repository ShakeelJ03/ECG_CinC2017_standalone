Step 1

Understand the task and the grading

The question. What exactly do we hand in, and what earns the marks?

My thinking

Before touching code I read the rubric, the professor's two emails, START_HERE.md, HOLDOUT_EVALUATION.md and REPORT_GUIDE.md. Two things shaped everything after.

Marks come from evidence, not from the score. Criterion 7 asks for controlled comparisons (one change at a time), with spread, at least one idea that failed, and the Noisy class handled on purpose. Criterion 9 asks for at least one decision we went back and changed. So I planned the work as a list of small experiments, each against a named reference.

"An improvement" has to beat the noise. Farhad's email says: find out how much the score moves when nothing changes, and don't call anything smaller an improvement. So the harness had to measure that noise and compare pipelines on the same folds.

Step 3

Literature review: what worked in 2017?

The question. What did the winners do, and what score is a fair yardstick?

My thinking

I looked at the four entries tied at 0.83 (Teijeiro, Datta, Zabihi, Hong) and the challenge paper. They share three ingredients:

  1. Rhythm-irregularity features from good R-peaks. AF is "irregularly irregular", so beat-to-beat interval statistics carry most of the signal.
  2. P-wave and beat-shape features. The missing P wave is the other half of the AF definition, and many "Other" rhythms show up in beat shape.
  3. Signal quality. Noisy is a quality problem, not a rhythm.

Zabihi's random forest on hand-made features tied with deep networks, so the features matter more than the classifier. That told me where to spend the effort.

One trap: the Challenge's 0.83 is the mean F1 over N, A and O only. Our course metric is the 4-class macro-F1, which includes Noisy. To compare fairly I made the harness report both.

Step 4

Build a fair test harness

The question. How do we compare two pipelines so that the difference means something, and why did runs take 40 minutes?

My thinking

Speed. Each run used to re-parse every file and recompute every feature. Features of one recording don't depend on any other recording or on labels, so they can be computed once per setting and saved. I key the cache on the feature code version, the settings and a fingerprint of the data, so a change to any of them rebuilds it. The whole 21-run plan now takes about 4.5 minutes.

Fairness. The course evaluator gives one set of 5 folds. One set of folds is a single coin toss. I use 5-fold StratifiedGroupKFold grouped by record, repeated with 5 seeds: 25 folds, the same 25 for every pipeline. Then I can compare two pipelines fold by fold.

Noise. The reference pipeline is scored 5 times (one per seed). How much those 5 scores differ is the noise floor: what changes when nothing changes. A change counts as a real gain only if its mean gain beats both the noise floor and twice its standard error.

experiments/harness.py — the same 25 splits for everyone
def folds(self):
    out = []
    for rep in range(N_REPEATS):                       # 5 seeds
        cv = StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=rep)
        for tr, te in cv.split(np.zeros(len(self.y)), self.y, self.groups):
            assert not set(self.groups[tr]) & set(self.groups[te]), "record leak"
            out.append((rep, tr, te))
    return out
the decision rule
d = np.array(new["fold_macro_f1"]) - np.array(ref["fold_macro_f1"])   # paired, fold by fold
se = d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(d))
noise = np.std(ref["repeat_pooled_macro_f1"], ddof=1)                # seed-only wobble
verdict = "real gain" if d.mean() > max(2*se, noise) else \
          "real loss" if d.mean() < -max(2*se, noise) else "no detectable difference"

What came out

On the supplied baseline the five repeats scored 0.649, 0.630, 0.624, 0.680, 0.648. That spread (SD 0.022) is pure seed noise. Across single folds the SD is even larger, 0.071.

So a "+0.01 improvement" on one run means nothing here. That's why every row on this page has a verdict next to it.

Inside every fold, the scaler, any feature selector, SMOTE and the classifier are fitted on the training part only. Only per-recording feature extraction is done once up front, which is safe because it never looks at other records or at labels.

Decision. All 21 experiments run through this one harness, with the rule fixed in advance.

Step 6

Look at the spectrum before filtering anything

The question. Is there actually mains hum or baseline wander to remove?

My thinking

The default instinct is "band-pass 0.5–40 Hz plus a 50 Hz notch". Farhad asked us to look first and filter only what is there. So I computed a Welch spectrum for every record and plotted the median per class.

What I looked for: a sharp line at 50 or 60 Hz (mains hum), and a big bump below 0.5 Hz (baseline wander).

Result: no line at 50 or 60 Hz in any class, in 0 of 431 records. Power falls steeply above 40 Hz, because the AliveCor device already band-limits the signal before it's stored. Wander below 0.5 Hz is only about 2 % of power.

This also means the supplied "49–51 Hz powerline" feature measures nearly nothing on this data.

What came out

Decision. There is little to filter. I'd still test the filters (next step), but now I expect no gain.

Step 8

Fix the R-peak detector (E02)

The question. Are the R-peaks the baseline features are built on actually correct?

My thinking

I read the supplied _rpeaks(): band-pass 5–15 Hz, square the derivative, smooth, then keep peaks above 30 % of the single largest peak in the whole record. One electrode pop that's 10× taller than the beats sets the threshold so high that every real beat is missed.

A quick count confirmed it: the supplied detector found under 40 bpm in 19 % of Normal records. Normal sinus rhythm under 40 bpm is very rare, so those are missed beats.

I wrote the Pan–Tompkins logic properly: adaptive signal and noise levels that update beat by beat, a 200 ms refractory period, T-wave rejection, and search-back at half threshold when a beat seems missing.

tracks/ecg_team.py — rpeaks_pan_tompkins(), the adaptive part
if v > thr:                                   # candidate is a beat
    ...
    spki = 0.125 * v + 0.875 * spki           # signal level follows the beats
else:
    npki = 0.125 * v + 0.875 * npki           # noise level follows the rest
thr = npki + 0.25 * (spki - npki)             # threshold adapts every candidate
# search-back: no beat for 1.66 x mean RR -> take the best candidate above thr/2

The dataset has no beat annotations, so to validate it I compared both detectors with an independent, established one (wfdb's XQRS). The table on the right is that check.

Step 9

Rhythm features for AF (E03a)

The question. Can richer RR-interval features separate AF from Other and Normal?

My thinking

The Poincaré plot (each RR interval against the next) shows the idea. Normal sits in a tight cluster on the diagonal. AF is a cloud. Other shows separate islands (ectopic beats, bigeminy).

So I added 21 features that each measure one aspect of this picture:

  • robust spread: MAD and percentile range, which one missed beat can't blow up the way it does SDNN
  • Poincaré SD1, SD2 and SD1/SD2
  • successive-difference fractions: beats changing by more than 10 % or 20 %
  • sample entropy and COSEn: built for short AF strips
  • Lorenz-plot occupancy: how many cells of the ΔRR plot are filled
  • lag-1 autocorrelation of ΔRR: strongly negative in bigeminy, near zero in AF
  • premature-beat fraction and min/max heart rate
tracks/ecg_team.py — rr_features(), a few of the 21
sd1 = np.std(d) / np.sqrt(2)                          # d = successive RR differences
sd2 = np.sqrt(max(2 * np.var(rr) - np.var(d) / 2, 1e-12))
se  = _sampen(rr, m=1, r=0.2 * np.std(rr))            # sample entropy
pts = np.stack([d[:-1], d[1:]], 1)                    # Lorenz plot of ΔRR
occupied = len({tuple(c) for c in np.floor(pts / 0.04).astype(int)}) / len(pts)

Step 10

Other feature groups, and the rest of the pipeline (E03b–E09)

The question. Do beat shape, signal quality, wavelets, the envelope, a different spectrum, classifier, imbalance handling or feature selection add anything?

My thinking

Each group was first tried alone on top of E02, then the promising ones on top of the rhythm features. The course asks for features from at least two domains, so I built several: time-domain morphology, signal-quality indices, wavelet sub-band energies, and a Hilbert-envelope periodicity measure.

Morphology helped a lot on its own (+0.051). But once the rhythm features were in, it added only +0.008, which is below the noise. The likely reason is that both partly capture the same information, and 40 AF and 40 Noisy records are too few to show a small extra gain.

The model stage changed nothing detectable. That matches the literature: with good features, the classifier matters little. Feature selection was slightly worse in 18 of 25 folds, so there's no reason to add a stage.

Each of these runs is still useful evidence: the rubric explicitly wants ideas that didn't help.

Step 11

Go back and revisit (E10–E12)

The question. Was any earlier conclusion based on something we have since fixed?

My thinking

Step 8 showed the old detector breaks easily. Then I asked: which earlier results depended on it? E01c did. "Denoising hurts" was measured with the fragile detector, and filtering changes the signal's peak heights, which moves that detector's single global threshold.

So I re-ran denoising with the robust detector and the rhythm features (E10). The loss disappeared, from −0.029 to −0.003. I also tried denoising the rhythm path while computing quality features on the raw strip (E11), because cleaning can erase what the Noisy class is about. That was +0.008: no detectable difference.

E12 asks the reverse question: what if we keep the new rhythm features but use the old detector? Macro-F1 drops by 0.119 and Noisy collapses. That's direct proof that everything rests on the R-peaks.

This is the rubric's Criterion 9: a later result (E12 and the XQRS check) sent us back to an earlier decision (E01c), and we changed the conclusion. Preprocessing stays off, but now because the spectrum shows nothing to remove, not because filtering is harmful.

Step 13

Check that it all reproduces

The question. If a grader re-runs the code, do they get the same numbers?

My thinking

I copied the whole project into a fresh folder, deleted every cache and re-ran all 21 experiments. 20 of 21 matched to the last digit. E09 was slightly different between runs.

The cause: the course's mutual-information selector calls mutual_info_classif without a random seed, so it adds random noise differently every run. I fixed it in our track and re-ran from clean twice: now all 21 runs are identical. That's evidence for Criterion 4 (reproducibility).

tracks/ecg_team.py — select_features(), the seeded version
if str(cfg.get("select", "none")).lower() in ("mutual_info", "mi"):
    score = partial(mutual_info_classif, random_state=int(cfg.get("seed", 0)))
    return SelectKBest(score, k=k).fit(X, y)

What came out

I also checked that with all settings at their defaults, our extract_features() gives exactly the supplied features (max difference 0.0). That's what makes E00 a true baseline inside our own code.

And I dry-ran the final step: final.py expected writes EXPECTED_SCORE.md, and final.py predict refuses to run until that file is committed.

Decision. Everything on this page is reproducible from the repo with the lock file.
